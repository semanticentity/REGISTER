Play It Again

Music saves writing by saying "again". A program does the same: it goes back and runs the same lines again, and each time round it can decide what to do.

In this lesson (about 25 minutes, sound on). You will be able to:
  • write a loop that fills a whole row of steps in a few lines
  • spread any number of hits evenly over a bar, using a branch
  • explain how a program decides which line runs next
You will use what lessons 1 to 6 taught: loops, the tresillo, pointers, registers, and x366 instructions.
It keeps playing while you scroll.

Hear it: again and again

Press PLAY. A kick on every beat, a hi-hat on every eighth note. Lesson 1 called a section that plays again from the start a loop.

Sheet music saves ink the same way. A drum part writes one bar once and puts a repeat sign at each end, a thick line with two dots, meaning "play this again". more Written out in text, the kick looks like this, with x for a hit:

|: x . . . x . . . x . . . x . . . :|

Sixteen steps, written once, played as many times as the song needs.

Hear it: hits spread evenly

Here is another way to make a rhythm. Pick how many hits, and how many slots to spread them over. The page spaces them as evenly as it can, starting with a hit in the first slot, and puts them on the kick.

It starts on 4 hits over 16 sixteenth notes: four on the floor. Now pick 3 hits over 8 eighth notes. That is the tresillo from lesson 2. Try 5 over 8, then 7 over 16.

A rhythm made by spreading a number of hits over a number of slots as evenly as they will go is a Euclidean rhythm. more The computer scientist Godfried Toussaint noticed in 2004 that rhythms from all over the world are made this way, and named them after Euclid's algorithm, a method from ancient Greece that can do the spreading. Four on the floor and the tresillo are both Euclidean.

Going back

In lesson 6 every program ran straight down, one line after another. Eight hi-hat hits that way take eight store lines that all say nearly the same thing. Instead, a program can go back up and run the same lines again.

First it needs a name for the place to go back to. A name with a colon after it, marking a line of the program, is a label. more Below, again: is a label.

An instruction that makes the processor carry on from somewhere other than the next line is a jump. more JMP again means "carry on from the label again".

MOV BX, 0x1300   ; BX points at the hi-hat's step 0
MOV AL, 1
again:
MOV [BX], AL     ; a hit where BX points
ADD BX, 2        ; two steps on
JMP again        ; and back up

BX is a pointer, from lesson 5: MOV [BX], AL stores AL at whatever address BX holds. x366 gives each register two names. AX, BX, CX and DX hold two bytes, enough for an address. AL, BL, CL and DL are the same registers, used a byte at a time.

There is a problem: nothing ever stops it. After the hi-hat's 16 steps, BX walks on through the rest of the chip, writing 1 everywhere. On the real MTMC-16 it keeps going until it runs off the end of memory and the computer halts it with an error.

Counting down

The fix is to count. A register that keeps track of how many times are left is a counter. more x366 uses CX as its counter, with one instruction made for it: LOOP again takes 1 from CX, then goes back to again if CX is not 0 yet.


  

  

  
Press CLEAR DRUMS, then ONE LINE again and again. Each time round, CX goes down by 1 and BX goes up by 2. When CX reaches 0, LOOP stops going back and the program ends. Eight hits from seven lines, and sixteen would take no more.

  
Now change three numbers so the same program puts four on the floor on the kick: a hit on every beat, from 0x1000.

  


  
Deciding

  
Back to Euclidean rhythms. The page above spread its hits with a trick you could do with a jar. Every slot, pour in as many cups as there are hits. The jar holds as many cups as there are slots. Whenever it is full, play a hit and pour one jarful out. Start the jar at slots minus hits, so the first pour fills it and the first slot gets a hit.

Every slot, the program has to decide: is the jar full? CMP DL, 8 compares the jar in DL with 8. A jump that only happens when a test passes is a branch. more JL skip means "if it was less, carry on from skip". A jar that is not full yet goes around the hit.

Hear it again

  
A repeat sign and LOOP do the same job: go back to a marked place and play on. The chip works this way too. It plays steps 0 to 15, then carries on from step 0 again. The loop from lesson 1 works like a jump back at the end of every bar.

  
And a Euclidean rhythm is a loop with a choice inside: every slot, hit or no hit. The tresillo you wrote as bits in lesson 2 came out of a jar: three hits, eight slots, and one branch. Change two numbers and the same lines make any of them.

  
Every program on this page runs on the real MTMC-16 too, with .MEMORY 16K on the first line, and stores the same bytes.


  
  
Check yourself

  
Four on the floor is Euclidean too: 4 hits over 16 sixteenth notes. Change the jar program so it makes four on the floor that way, visiting every step. Work out every number first: where the jar starts, how much to pour, how big the jar is, how many slots to visit, and how far BX moves each time.

  

  
Now answer these out loud before you open them.

  
CX is 3 and the processor reaches LOOP again. What happens?
CX becomes 2. That is not 0, so the program goes back to the label again and runs those lines another time. See "Counting down".

  
What would the hi-hat program do with JMP and no counter?
Never stop. It would keep storing hits further and further along, past the hi-hat and through the rest of memory. See "Going back".

  
What is the difference between JMP and JL?
JMP always jumps. JL only jumps when the last compare found the register was less, so it is a branch. See "Deciding".

  
Why does the jar start at slots minus hits?
So that the first pour fills it exactly, and the rhythm starts with a hit in the first slot. See "Deciding".
