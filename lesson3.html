Notes Are Numbers

Every key on a piano has a number. Once you know it, moving a tune up or down is just adding.

In this lesson (about 15 minutes, sound on). You will be able to:
  • find any key's note number by counting from middle C
  • transpose a melody by adding or subtracting
  • explain why adding 12 sounds like the same note, higher
You will use what lessons 1 and 2 taught: steps, rests, bytes, addresses and POKE.
It keeps playing while you scroll.

Hear it: high and low

Press PLAY. No drums this time, just one voice, slower than before. Some sounds are higher and some are lower. How high or low a sound is, is its pitch. more

Notes played one after another, so they make a tune, are a melody. more This one is the opening of Beethoven's "Ode to Joy", from 1824. It plays on every other step, with a rest between notes.

Now play the keys below. They are one stretch of a piano, every key in order, white and black. The white key on the far left is middle C, the C near the middle of a piano and the usual place to start counting. more

Going from one key to the very next, black keys included, is a semitone: the smallest step between two notes. more Count the keys from middle C to the C on the far right. That is 12 semitones.

Play those two Cs one after the other. They sound like the same note, one higher than the other. That distance, 12 semitones, is an octave. more

Now press HIGHER a few times while the melody plays, then LOWER, then ORIGINAL.

It is still the same tune each time, just starting from a different note. Moving a whole melody up or down by the same number of semitones is transposing it. more Each press moved every note 2 semitones.

A note is a number

Now the computer side. The chip can't store "E" or "the C near the middle". It stores bytes. So it gives every key a note number: middle C is 60, and each semitone up adds 1. more Nearly all music software uses these same numbers, from a standard called MIDI.

A rule like that, which turns things into numbers so a computer can store them, is an encoding. more Text works the same way. The usual rule says the letter A is 65. Once everyone agrees on the rule, a number can mean a note, a letter, or anything else.

Moving a tune is arithmetic

Here is the payoff. One semitone up is +1. One octave up is +12, because an octave is 12 semitones. So to transpose a melody, add the same number to every note.

The melody lives in the lead voice's boxes, $1100 to $110F, one box per step. Each box holds a note number, and 0 means rest. Here are the same boxes, as numbers:

Under the boxes are the note names. Once you move the melody, some notes land on black keys. A black key is named after the white key just below it, plus a sharp, written ♯, which means one semitone up. more So F♯ is the black key just above F. The number after the name says which octave: E4 is the E just above middle C.

Transpose it up an octave. Add 12 to each note with a POKE line. The first line is done for you: step 0 holds 64, and 64 + 12 = 76. Press ORIGINAL above first if you moved it.

One limit from lesson 2: a box holds a byte, and a byte stops at 255. Try POKE $1100, 300 and read what the console says.

Hear it again

Listen to the melody an octave up. Same tune, brighter. But why does adding 12 sound like the same notes?

A pitch is really a frequency: how many times per second the sound vibrates. more It is counted in hertz, written Hz, which means vibrations per second. more Every octave up doubles the frequency, and that is what your ear hears as "the same note, higher".

Note numberNoteFrequency
57A, below middle C220 Hz
60middle C261.6 Hz
69A, above middle C440 Hz
81A, an octave higher880 Hz

So the note number counts semitones in a straight line, 1, 2, 3, and the chip turns it into a frequency that doubles every 12. Here is the rule it uses, in words: start at 440 Hz for note 69, and multiply by 2 once for every 12 notes above it.

frequency = 440 × 2 ^ ((note − 69) ÷ 12)

note 69  →  440 Hz
note 81  →  440 × 2  =  880 Hz   (12 above)
note 57  →  440 ÷ 2  =  220 Hz   (12 below)

Remember lesson 1's click track? The high click was 84 and the low clicks were 72. 84 − 72 = 12. They were an octave apart all along.

Check yourself

Put the original melody on the bass voice, two octaves lower. The bass voice's boxes start at $1200, on the same steps as the lead. Work out each number first, then POKE them.

Now answer these out loud before you open them.

Middle C is 60. What is the C two octaves up?

84: 60 + 12 + 12. That was the high click in lesson 1. See "Moving a tune is arithmetic".

A melody is 60, 64, 67. What is it transposed down an octave?

48, 52, 55. Take 12 from each note. See "Moving a tune is arithmetic".

Why does POKE refuse a note of 300?

A box holds one byte, and a byte stops at 255. See lesson 2, "Eight bits make a byte".

Note 69 is 440 Hz. What is note 57?

220 Hz. 57 is 12 below 69, an octave down, and an octave down halves the frequency. See "Hear it again".
