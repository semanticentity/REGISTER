Memory and POKE

Make a drum groove by writing numbers into a computer, and find out how a program talks to a sound chip.

In this lesson (about 15 minutes, sound on). You will be able to:
  • count a bar of music in sixteenths
  • turn on a drum hit at any step by writing one number into memory
  • explain why writing memory makes sound
It keeps playing while you scroll.

Hear it: counting to sixteen

Press PLAY and listen. You hear a metronome: a click that keeps steady time. more

Each click is a beat, the steady pulse you tap your foot to. more Tap along.

The clicks come in groups of four, and the high one starts each group. A group of beats is a bar, also called a measure. Most pop and dance music has four beats in a bar. more

Musicians split each beat into four smaller pieces. Each piece is a sixteenth note, because a bar of four beats holds sixteen of them. more Count them out loud with the clicks. The numbers land on the clicks, and e, &, a fall between them:

1 e & a  2 e & a  3 e & a  4 e & a

Each box above is one sixteenth. Click an empty box to add a click there, and click a lit box to remove it. Try adding a click on every &.

The sound chip in these lessons, REGISTER, counts the same way. It splits each bar into 16 steps, one per sixteenth note. more After the last step it goes back to the first and plays the bar again. Music that repeats like that is a loop. more

Memory is a row of numbered boxes

Now the computer side. A computer's memory is a long row of boxes, each holding one small number. more

Every box has an address: its number in the row. more Every box holds one byte: a whole number from 0 to 255. more

Addresses here are written in hex, a way of writing numbers that counts in sixteens, using the letters A to F for ten to fifteen. more A $ in front marks a hex number. For now you need only two facts: $1000 is the name of one box, and $100C is twelve boxes after it, because C means twelve. Lesson 2 shows why programmers like hex.

REGISTER owns the boxes from $1000 to $1FFF. On every step it reads some of them, and plays what it finds.

The kick drum is 16 boxes

The chip can play five sounds at once. Each one is a voice. more The first voice is the kick drum, the low boom at the bottom of most dance music. more

The kick gets 16 boxes, one per step, from $1000 to $100F. On each step the chip looks in that step's box. If it holds 1, the kick plays. If it holds 0, it stays quiet.

One catch: musicians count from 1, and computers count from 0. The first step is step 0, in box $1000. So beat 1 is step 0, beat 2 is step 4, beat 3 is step 8, and beat 4 is step 12.

Your first groove is four on the floor: a kick drum on every beat. more It drives house and disco. Click boxes to build it. Each click writes a byte into memory, and you hear the change the next time the bar comes around.

POKE: write a box by its address

Clicking a box is a shortcut. Underneath, every click is a POKE: put a value into the box at an address. more Its partner is PEEK: read the value in the box at an address. more Home computers in the 1980s had both commands built in, and people used them to make their machines play music.

POKE $1000, 1    ; step 0: kick on
POKE $1004, 1    ; step 4: kick on
PEEK $1004       ; read it back

To find a step's box, add the step number to the voice's first address. Step 4 is $1000 + 4 = $1004. Press RUN on the lines below, then change them.

The second voice is the snare drum, the bright crack that answers the kick. more Its boxes are $1020 to $102F. Same rule: one box per step, and 1 means hit.

Pop music puts a backbeat over the kick: the snare on beats 2 and 4. more Which steps are those? Write it with POKE lines in the box above.

Hear it again

Listen to what you built. The kick is on every beat, and the snare is on 2 and 4. That is the skeleton of most pop music, and it is six bytes:

POKE $1000, 1    ; kick,  beat 1 (step 0)
POKE $1004, 1    ; kick,  beat 2 (step 4)
POKE $1008, 1    ; kick,  beat 3 (step 8)
POKE $100C, 1    ; kick,  beat 4 (step 12)
POKE $1024, 1    ; snare, beat 2 (step 4)
POKE $102C, 1    ; snare, beat 4 (step 12)

Look at the addresses. Each beat is 4 boxes after the last, because a beat is four sixteenths. Counting music in fours and adding 4 to an address are the same idea.

The clicks from the start were bytes too. They live in the third voice's boxes, starting at $1100. Each lit box holds a number for how high the click sounds: 84 for the high click, 72 for the low ones. A later lesson explains those numbers.

There is no "play a click" command anywhere. The program writes memory, and the chip reads memory. A device you control that way is memory-mapped. more Game consoles like the NES worked this way, and so does the MTMC-16 this chip is built for.

Check yourself

One more groove, and no clicking this time. Drummers often add a kick just before the bar repeats, on the & of beat 4. Count it: beat 4, e, &.

Now answer these out loud before you open them.

Why are the kicks for beats 1 to 4 four boxes apart?

Each beat holds four sixteenths, and each sixteenth is one box. See "Hear it again".

What does POKE $1008, 0 do?

It writes 0 into the box for step 8, so the kick on beat 3 goes quiet. See "The kick drum is 16 boxes".

What would you POKE to put the snare on beat 3?

Beat 3 is step 8, and the snare starts at $1020. $1020 + 8 = $1028, so POKE $1028, 1. See "POKE: write a box by its address".

Why doesn't the chip need a "play kick" command?

It is memory-mapped. It reads its boxes on every step, so writing 1 in the right box is the command. See the end of "Hear it again".
