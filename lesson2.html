Bits and Patterns

A drum pattern is a row of on and off switches, and so is a byte. Learn to read each one as the other.

In this lesson (about 15 minutes, sound on). You will be able to:
  • hear a rhythm as hits and rests
  • read a byte in binary and work out its value
  • write any 16-step rhythm as four hex digits, one per beat
You will use what lesson 1 taught: beats, bars, sixteenths, steps, bytes and hex.

Hear it: hits and rests

Press PLAY. The kick and snare from lesson 1 are back, with a new sound on top. Together they make a rhythm: the pattern of when sounds happen, and when they don't. more

Look at the boxes below. A step with a 1 is a hit. A step with a 0 is a rest: a step where nothing plays. more Rests matter as much as hits. Without them there is no rhythm, only noise.

The new sound is a hi-hat, the ticking cymbal that keeps time over the drums. more The chip's fifth voice, the noise voice, plays it here. It hits every other step: on each beat and each &.

A note half a beat long is an eighth note, because a bar of four beats holds eight of them. more The hi-hat is playing eighth notes. Count them: 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &.

Now press NEW KICK, and listen to the kick change.

The kick now hits on 1, on the & of 2, and on 4. A hit placed between the beats, where you don't expect it, is syncopation. more It is what makes a rhythm lean forward.

This kick pattern is the tresillo: three hits spaced 3, 3 and 2 eighth notes apart. more It sits at the heart of Cuban music, and you hear it all over pop and dance music today. Press FOUR ON THE FLOOR to switch back and compare.

Every box is a switch

Every box in those rows holds 0 or 1. A single 0 or 1 is a bit, the smallest piece a computer stores. more So a rhythm is a row of bits: 1 for a hit, 0 for a rest.

Eight bits make a byte

A byte, the number in one box, is eight bits side by side. Writing numbers with only 0 and 1 is binary, where each place is worth twice the place to its right. more The eight places are worth 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2 and 1. Add up the places that are on, and you have the byte's value. Click the switches.

1286432168421

Turn every switch on and you get 128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 255. That is why a byte stops at 255: there are no more places.

Four bits, one beat

Now split the bits into groups of four. A group of four bits is a nibble, half a byte. more Four bits count from 0000 to 1111, which is 0 to 15. Hex has exactly 16 digits, 0 to 9 and then A to F, so one hex digit is exactly one nibble. That is why programmers write in hex: you can read the bits straight off it.

1000 = 8      0010 = 2      1010 = A
0100 = 4      0001 = 1      1111 = F

Here is the musical part. A beat is four sixteenths, and a nibble is four bits. So in a 16-step pattern, each hex digit is one beat, and four hex digits are a whole bar.

Click steps below and watch each beat's hex digit change. Or type four hex digits, pick a voice, and press LOAD to copy the pattern into that voice's 16 boxes.

Hear it again

Now you can read a rhythm straight from its hex. Each digit says where the hits fall inside one beat:

DigitBitsHits in that beat
81000on the beat
20010on the &, between beats
A1010on the beat and the &: two eighth notes
F1111all four sixteenths
00000a whole beat of rest
$8888   kick:   four on the floor, every beat
$0808   snare:  backbeat, beats 2 and 4
$AAAA   hi-hat: eighth notes, every 1 and &
$8208   kick:   tresillo, 1, the & of 2, and 4

Look at the tresillo, $8208. Beat 1 is an 8, a hit on the beat. Beat 2 is a 2, a hit on the &: that is the syncopation, written as one digit. Beat 3 is a 0, a whole beat of rest. You can see the groove without hearing it.

Check yourself

House music often moves the hi-hat off the beat: a hit on every & and nowhere else. Work out its four hex digits on paper first, then type them, pick the hi-hat, and LOAD.

Now answer these out loud before you open them.

What is 1111 1111 in everyday numbers?

255. All eight places are on: 128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 1. See "Eight bits make a byte".

Why is one hex digit exactly one beat here?

A hex digit is four bits, a beat is four sixteenths, and each sixteenth is one bit. See "Four bits, one beat".

What would $F000 sound like on the snare?

Four quick hits on beat 1, then three beats of rest: a short drum roll. F is 1111, and each 0 is a silent beat.

Sixteen bits fit in two bytes. So why does the chip spend sixteen boxes on a pattern, one per step?

So a box can hold more than on or off, like the pitch numbers in lesson 1's click track, and so one POKE changes one step without any bit math. Saving space and keeping things simple pull against each other; this chip picks simple.
